Western Water Dashboard

For reporters

This page is for reporters and editors who use figures from this site. It tells you how to cite a figure, what each main figure means, and who to contact. The methods page gives the full method for every number.

How to cite a figure

Credit line: Source: [provider] data, shown on the Western Water Dashboard (buschbrian.github.io/western-water-dashboard).

Name the provider first, because the measurements are theirs. Each reservoir page names its provider under "Measured by". This site shows the readings and does arithmetic with them.

  • Give the date of the reading. Each reservoir page shows it as the reading date. The numbers change every morning. Some providers publish only once a month.
  • Link to the page that shows the figure. Each reservoir has its own page, for example Lake Powell. The address stays the same from day to day.
  • Copy the address of a map view. The storage map and the precipitation, snowpack and drought pages keep the place you chose in the address.

What the main figures mean

Percent full
Storage now, divided by the reservoir's full level. The reservoir page names the full level it uses.
Normal for this week
The middle value for the same week in earlier years. The page names the period. It is 1991 through 2020, or 2015 through last year if you choose that period.
History rank
This ranks today's reading against readings near the same date in each earlier year. "Lowest of 12 years" means the lowest of this year and 11 earlier years. The record starts in 2015. Do not write "record low". Write "lowest for this time of year since 2015".
Change in 30 days, change in 1 year
The difference between the newest reading and the reading nearest that many days before it.
At its lowest in 4 months
A reservoir that reports every day shows its gain since its lowest reading in the last 120 days. The page shows the date of that low.
Drought
The share of land in a drainage area at each class of the U.S. Drought Monitor's weekly map. The Drought Monitor publishes a new map each Thursday.
Snow
Snow water, compared with the 1991 through 2020 median for the same day. Early in the season the usual amount is very small. Then the page gives the depth in inches instead of a percentage.
River flow
The daily mean flow at a U.S. Geological Survey gauge. Dams upstream of some gauges store and release water. The page says which gauges it compares with normal.

What the figures cannot support

  • Nothing here is a forecast. Every number is a measurement, a published assessment, or arithmetic on those.
  • People operate these reservoirs. A reservoir can fall because its operator releases water for deliveries or power. Ask the operator before you write that a watershed is drying.
  • The period you compare against changes the answer. Name the period when you give a percent of normal.
  • Readings are provisional. A provider can revise a reading later. The next morning's data carries the revision.
  • A combined total mixes reading dates. The storage charts page gives the range of dates behind each total.
  • Read the notes on a water level. The Lake Powell and Lake Mead pages show a water level. Read the note on the page before you quote it.
  • This is not an official product. Where this site and an agency disagree, the agency is right.

Data files

The public data API gives the same numbers as JSON files, with a description of each field. Each reservoir page also has a CSV download of that reservoir's readings.
